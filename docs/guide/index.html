0. How damage works

Everything below follows from the damage formula, so here it is without luck:

damage % = base × (your HP / 10) × (100 − their stars × their HP) / 100, rounded down.

Two details do most of the work:

  • Both HP values are the displayed HP, 1 to 10. A unit on 51 internal HP shows 6 and hits like a 6, same as a unit on 59. Only crossing a multiple of 10 changes anything.
  • Stars are multiplied by the defender's displayed HP. Each star cuts 1% per HP. A full unit on a city (3 stars) takes 30% less; a unit on 4 HP on the same city takes only 12% less. Terrain protects healthy units much more than hurt ones.

A counterattack uses the same formula with the defender's HP after it was hit. That's why hitting first is so strong: you hit at full strength, they hit back weakened.

1. Ending your turn in enemy range

If you end your turn next to, or in reach of, an enemy of equal strength, it gets to attack you first. Whether that loses you the fight depends on the tile you're on and the tile it attacks from.

Each table below plays this out exactly. Your unit is on the row's tile. The enemy attacks from the column's tile. After that, the two units take turns attacking each other until one is destroyed, with ammo counted and your own city healing you 2 HP at the start of each of your turns. Each cell shows who wins, how much HP the winner has left, and both units' HP right after the first attack and counter.

Woods are no better than plains here. A full-HP hit for 55% base: on plains you lose 49 HP (55 × 0.9, rounded down) and drop to 51, which shows as 6. On woods you lose 44 and drop to 56, which still shows as 6. Your counter is the same either way, so the fight goes the same way. Why 3 stars win, and only if they're on 2 or fewer. On a city you drop to 7. Your counter as a 7 against a unit on plains does 34 (55 × 0.7 × 0.9), so it drops to 66, which also shows as 7. Now it's 7 against 7 and it's your turn, so you're the one hitting first in an even fight. If they attack from a city instead, the same counter does 26 (55 × 0.7 × 0.7), they stay on 8, and you're still a point behind on your turn. Their tile matters as much as yours because it softens your counter.
Your own city always wins. After the first exchange you're at worst 7 against 8. At the start of your turn the city heals you 2 to 9, and you attack first against a unit you now have more HP than.

An md tank can end its turn in tank range anywhere. A tank does 15% base to an md tank; an md tank does 85% to a tank. If the tank hits first on plains it takes the md tank to 87 (shows 9). The md tank's counter as a 9 takes the tank to 32 (shows 4). The first attack is worth a point or two of HP; a gap like 85 against 15 is worth far more. A tank never wins by ending its turn in md tank range, not even on its own city.
A mech can only end its turn in tank range on its own city. Tanks hit mechs with their machine gun at 70% base, harder than a mech's bazooka hits a tank (55%). A tank attacking first takes a mech on plains to 37 (shows 4), and the mech's counter as a 4 barely scratches it. Even on a mountain the mech drops to 6 against the tank's 8. Only the city heal closes the gap. The other way round, a tank can end its turn in mech range on plains or better. The mech attacks first, but the tank's machine gun out-trades the bazooka, unless the mech is on a mountain or its own city.
Infantry should never attack a full anti-air. Infantry do 5% base to anti-air. Anti-air does 105% to infantry, so its counter from 10 HP kills infantry on a road outright and leaves 1 HP on plains. For the same reason anti-air can end its turn in infantry or recon range anywhere.
Infantry can only end its turn in range of infantry (on a city) and mechs (on its own city). Against everything else the damage gap is too big for any tile to fix. Infantry advance where nothing can reach them that turn, or as bait you expect to lose.

3. Maps

From the tables above:

  • Woods between two armies don't give cover in even fights. For the common 55% fights they play exactly like plains, so a forest no-man's land is open ground.
  • Pushes happen where 3-star tiles inside enemy range face 2-star-or-worse tiles. That's the only place an equal unit can end its turn in range and win. Count those tiles for each side to check a map's balance: the side with more of them has more places to push.
  • Roads hurt whoever uses them to approach. 0 stars means being hit to 5 instead of 6.
  • A front-line city is worth more than its income. For equal units your own city can't lose a fight, because of the heal. Taking one from the enemy turns its fortress into yours.

4. Standoffs

If neither side has a good tile to end its turn on in the other's range, neither should do it. And nothing forces you to: you can always end your turn without moving. Unlike chess there's no position where you have to make a losing move.

The exact solver shows this on small boards. Infantry against infantry, and tank against tank, on empty 4x4 and 5x5 maps are draws with perfect play: both sides wait outside each other's range forever. On 3x3 the corners are already in range on turn one, so the first player attacks first and wins. Put a city or an HQ on the board and the first player wins too: waiting now costs something, and the first player gets there first.

A unit whose reach is longer than the enemy's threat range can break a standoff: rockets fire at 5 tiles and infantry threaten 4, so rockets can hit infantry that can't reach back.

5. Tempo and value

Moving first is worth about 3000 funds. The same engine played both sides on symmetric 12x12 maps with one base each. The first player won most decisive games, because it gets the first capture of every contested city and the first attack in every fight it picks. Giving the second player extra starting funds found the break-even (decisive games, first player against second): +0: 20-8, +2000: 18-10, +3000: 31-25, +4000: 10-18. That's three infantry.

What units are worth. The engine has a value for each unit type, as a share of its cost. A tuner keeps nudging those values and keeping whatever wins more self-play games. Where they settled: infantry 198%, tank 139%, anti-air 107%, mech 104%, neotank 96%, artillery 90%, recon 85%, rockets 81%, md tank 73%. Measured on small and medium maps at fast time controls, so this is value to this engine's play. It fits section 2: an md tank beats a tank one on one, but two tanks cost less than one md tank and can fight in two places.

With a day limit, a bigger army doesn't count. The day limit is decided by property count, so material only matters if it turns into captures or the enemy HQ before the end.

6. Not known yet

  • Whole-game strategy (build orders, when to push). The engine isn't strong enough yet for its choices to count as findings. The next step is to look through its games for what the winners do differently.
  • Fog. You can't attack what you can't see, and infantry see 2 tiles while threatening 4. So under fog you could end your turn in range without being seen, which turns section 1 around. To be solved on small boards.
  • Artillery and rockets in exact solves.
  • No day limit, with the HQ and rout as the only ways to win.
  • Fights of two or three units a side, where the order of attacks matters.

7. Problems with no-fog AW

Measured where marked, otherwise opinion.

  1. Waiting is rewarded (measured on small boards). Combat on its own doesn't force a result; income, captures and the clock do.
  2. Moving first is worth about 3000 funds (measured).
  3. Woods don't matter in the most common even fights (measured). The 2-star tile barely changes them.
  4. Day-limit games come down to counting properties. It ends the game without deciding it.
  5. With no luck, a lot of fights are decided before they start. Good for analysis, less fun to play.
